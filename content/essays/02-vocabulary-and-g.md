A recent thread here featured a philosophy PhD with a VCI near 145 asking whether his vocabulary should count toward his reasoning ability. The question is more interesting than it looks because vocabulary occupies a strange position in the taxonomy. It is among the most g-loaded subtests in any Wechsler-style battery, and it is also the most sensitive to education, culture, language background, and age. Both halves are true, and most people only know one of them.

The g-loading first. Jensen's explanation, which I find convincing, is that vocabulary is acquired mostly by inference from context, rather than by instruction. Nobody teaches you the ten thousandth word you know. You infer its meaning from the sentences it appears in, dozens of times, until the estimate converges. A large lexicon is therefore a ledger of tens of thousands of small fluid acts, accumulated over a lifetime. Cattell's investment theory says the same thing from the other direction: crystallized ability is what fluid ability deposits when it is applied to experience. Vocabulary is the cleanest fossil record of that process we can measure.

Now the problem. A fossil record depends on the sediment. Reading exposure, schooling, socioeconomic environment, and whether the test's language is your first one all change how much deposits for a given amount of fluid ability. Age changes it as well, and this is the part the sub tends to miss: vocabulary keeps climbing into the late sixties in the large cross-sectional datasets while fluid reasoning peaks in the twenties. The same vocabulary score means something different at 25 and at 65, and a young adult with a high VCI is telling you something stronger than an older one with the same number.

There is also a distinction that matters for anyone trying to raise the score. Cramming word lists produces gains that behave like practice effects, real on the scale and hollow underneath. Reading produces the other kind, because comprehension-driven exposure is the very process that made the index g-loaded in the first place. Stanovich's Matthew effect describes the consequence: readers acquire vocabulary that makes reading easier, which produces more reading, and the gap compounds for decades.

Of note, vocabulary also behaves unusually well at the high range. The tail of word knowledge is long, so rare words remain discriminating where matrix items run out of room, and definitional formats load higher on g than recognition formats do. That leaves an open design question I don't think the field has settled: whether the construct worth measuring is the breadth of a lexicon or the precision with which someone can arbitrate between near-synonyms. Breadth is what most tests count. However, precision may be what the g-loading was tracking all along.

As always, I'd like to know which parts of this people think are wrong or missing. Thanks for reading.
