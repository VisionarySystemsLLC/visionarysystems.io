47 started, 8 finished, 6 reached The Zenith: ongoing remarks from calibrating a high-range test

September 23, 2026. Originally posted to r/cognitiveTesting, where the discussion took place.

I happened across r/cognitiveTesting in early 2025 and became intrigued by the concept of measuring humans. In addition to intelligence measurement, I also thought the IPIP was cool, and research into the Big 5 has broadened my curiosity on that front since. Concurrently, I thought it would be worthwhile to embark on a quest for self-improvement, and psychometrics facilitated that at the time. I also thought certain aspects of neurodivergent ability were ignored by the current g-loading orthodoxy, so it fit my iconoclastic nature to build my own instrument. This is the third post in a loose series about what that has actually involved.

I have one note on that orthodoxy since it's a seed for later material I’d love to write about. G-loading is a great tool near the middle of the distribution, because that's where we can verify it. It becomes far less informative as you approach profoundly gifted territory, where the manifold loosens and abilities individuate, which is exactly where verification gets hardest. My suspicion, and one of the things I hope to investigate, is that there's a kind of “manifold variance” at work: neurodivergence and greater neural uniqueness may correspond to greater divergence among intelligences, as if certain neural structures lay the foundation for divergent outcomes in intelligence, personality, and beyond.

The instrument mentioned earlier is the Visionary Psychometric Scan (VPS), a 36-item, untimed, adaptive reasoning battery currently in beta calibration. It measures five coordinates: fluid reasoning, crystallized density, visuospatial reasoning, quantitative reasoning, and integrative intelligence.

The instrument has three tiers: The Gate (items 1 through 16), The Bridge (17 through 26), and The Zenith (27 through 36). Each functions on a domain-by-domain basis, and the design intent is that the coordinates scale roughly from 100 to 130 across The Gate, 130 to 145 across The Bridge, and 145 to 160 across The Zenith, assuming SD = 15. Of note, those are intended ranges rather than measured ones. The calibration exists to find out whether the design hit them, so I'm not claiming precision yet. The Gate starts near average difficulty and climbs quickly, so most takers strain from the first items. Advancing to The Bridge requires at least 8 of 16 correct in total, and 13 of 26 through The Bridge grants you access to The Zenith. That total-score gating is a limitation of the Beta; ideally, advancement would be measured per domain, with something like 3 of 4 correct in a domain at The Gate unlocking that domain's Bridge items. The intent is that The Gate discriminates around the gifted threshold, and whether it does is the purpose of the calibration.

So far, 47 people have started the Beta, and 8 have completed it. One of those completes is mine, some are from older versions of the test, and 2 were manually marked complete after the taker finished everything but the final click. Times vary with performance; some people take 3+ hours, and others finish in 30 minutes. One participant spent 88 minutes on a single item and solved it, which is a strong example of how much friction the test elicits from takers. Of the 10 people who started since my last post and didn't finish, here's where they stopped: 2 at the consent form, 1 at the consent acknowledgement, 1 at the data capture page, 1 at the instructions, 3 at Item 02, 1 at Item 08, and 1 at Item 14. In total, excluding my own run, 6 takers have reached The Zenith, and all 6 completed. Average-ability takers probably get overwhelmed quite quickly, which says more about the instrument's floor than about them.

I built the items with AI assistance, and the thought partnership sharpened the test far beyond what I could have managed alone. The items are unique to the VPS (not drawn from any existing test), and a guiding principle throughout was the desire to identify key abilities as opposed to just browsing for tasks with high g-loading. I had to comb through the whole thing as a pretend taker myself to make sure someone with no prior knowledge could tell what they were looking at. We worked from standardized IRT, and I'm considering delta-scoring for the norming phase, meaning partial credit scaled to the quality of a response (since certain distractors indicate more ability than others). Each item has 8 options, which lowers guessing efficacy, raises the information per item, and lets fewer items and fewer takers reach the same degree of precision. The untimed format shrinks the required sample further by reducing careless-error risk.

Three design choices set the VPS apart, and I'll only develop one of them here. The first is that no outside knowledge is required by the test, and a piece on that will appear at some point. The second is the inclusion of integrative intelligence (cross-domain reasoning) as a coordinate, treated as exploratory until the data earns more and deserving of its own write-up. The third is the absence of time limits, and it's the one I'll expand on. Cognitive proficiency clearly relates to reasoning, but the relationship is far too complex to be analyzed with CPI-specific tasks. My own working memory is stronger when applied to actual reasoning than to rote tasks. The WAIS tries to account for that by placing Arithmetic in WMI, but Arithmetic is reasoning-loaded, so it obfuscates the construct the other way. The SB6's decision to pull working memory out of the FSIQ moved in this direction as well. With that said, by removing time constraints and allowing a scratchpad, you essentially remove cognitive proficiency from the equation, permitting an undistorted view of someone's ability. I think it will produce a better instrument. Period. Cognitive proficiency is a human compute issue whereas reasoning has far greater capacity without the chains of compute, and that is particularly true for neurodivergent individuals, whose unique perception of reality comes with its own computation quirks. This means two things to me: high-range testing must shed cognitive proficiency altogether, and timed testing disadvantages neurodivergent takers. There's a synergistic effect as well, since neurodivergent individuals are more likely to have spikes in their profiles, and some high-range ability needs more time to manifest. A full piece on this decision is coming later as well.

The structure front-loads the forms, which can dissuade people from completion, and the data indicates that it is a powerful dissuader indeed. Inside the test and particularly in the test items, I deliberately reduced the visual scaffolding to leave the taker alone with the questions, which runs counter to what makes a test easy to start. I haven't changed either yet, even though I should consider at least shortening the on-ramp.

In case you take it: it's hard from the first items, plan on 45 to 90 minutes on a computer, most people won't clear The Gate, and the floor of the instrument is not the floor of the person. Details are on my profile if interested.

On content, the test appears to load semantic reasoning well and hits pattern recognition, structural detection, visuospatial manipulation, and abstract comprehension thoroughly. I do wonder whether analogical thinking is an absence of note, though. It's clearly g-loaded, but I'm not sure whether the other domains capture the same abilities sufficiently in a different form. As with the last two posts, I'd like to know which parts of this folks think are wrong or missing.
